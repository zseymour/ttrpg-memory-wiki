# Duet Play — The 1-on-1 Deep Dive

This reference covers the structural differences and required reflexes for running TTRPGs with a single human player. The skill's main SKILL.md covers the basics; this is the depth.

## Why duet is structurally different

Party play and duet are not the same game with one fewer player. They are different games.

| Dimension | Party play | Duet |
|---|---|---|
| Spotlight | Rotates among PCs | Always on the one PC |
| Pacing | Slowed by party caucus, banter, cross-talk | No pad; runs faster, demands quicker GM response |
| Combat safety | Party can take a hit; cleric heals; tank tanks | Solo PC has no buffer; one bad round can end campaign |
| Social density | NPCs spread among party members | All NPC weight on one PC |
| Player agency | Constrained by party consensus | Unconstrained; PC is sovereign |
| Tone | Tends toward heroic ensemble | Tends toward intimate, character-driven |
| Failure stakes | Group can recover | One PC's failure = full failure |
| GM stance | Adversarial / referee | Collaborator / co-author |

The biggest mental shift: **GM as collaborator, not adversary**. In party play, the GM provides opposition the party overcomes together. In duet, the GM is the player's writing partner. The world still pushes back, but the orientation is "we are making this story together."

## Combat for solo PCs

Solo combat is dangerous in ways CR and encounter math don't capture. Compensate using these dials, applied in combination:

### Dial 1 — Reduce enemy numbers
- 1 enemy at the PC's level (or lower with action-oriented adjustments).
- OR 2–4 enemies at much lower level.
- **Never more than 4 active simultaneously.** Use waves if needed.
- Action economy is the killer in solo combat — multiple low-CR enemies often hit harder than one same-level enemy.

### Dial 2 — Reduce enemy HP / damage
- HP within or below the listed Hit Dice range.
- Damage on the low end of the range (or median).
- Convert burst damage to grinding damage (instead of "30 damage in one shot," "10/10/10 across three rounds").

### Dial 3 — Use minion rules
For "epic feel" without TPK risk, throw 4–8 minions at the PC. Any damage = minion drops. The PC mows through them satisfyingly. Use for cinematic ambush scenes; reserve real threats for boss encounters.

### Dial 4 — Provide a sidekick
A companion NPC adds action economy without overshadowing the PC. See "Companion NPCs" below.

### Dial 5 — Telegraph everything
A single ambush can end a duet campaign. The Three Signposts rule is non-negotiable in duet. Telegraph monster abilities the round before they fire.

### Dial 6 — Avoid save-or-die
Convert save-or-die effects to conditions with timers. Petrification → "you're slowed; on a failed save next round, you stiffen further; full petrification on the third round." This gives the PC time to react.

### Dial 7 — Non-death failure states
The PC losing doesn't have to mean death. Consider:
- **Capture.** PC wakes in a cell, on a slave ship, in a temple of the cult.
- **Scarring.** PC takes a permanent injury — lost eye, lasting limp, trauma.
- **Debt.** PC is rescued by an NPC who now expects payment.
- **Oath.** PC is bound by a vow extracted in extremis.
- **Lost time.** PC blacks out / falls unconscious / is healed by strangers; weeks pass; the situation has changed.
- **Lost allies / objectives.** The PC survives but the goal is lost; an ally dies.

These create more story than death, especially mid-campaign.

### Dial 8 — Use the system's safety nets
- 5e: Inspiration, death saves, healing potions readily available.
- PF2e: hero points (per-session refresh).
- PbtA: built into the math; partial successes carry the load.
- Blades: Resistance Rolls + Stress; Armor + Special Armor; Devil's Bargains for +1d.
- Fate: fate points; concession.
- Cypher: GM Intrusion +XP, can be refused.

Lean on these aggressively. They are what the system gave you to manage difficulty without fudging.

## Companion NPCs (the sidekick problem)

The default fix for "duet PC has no support" is a companion NPC. This is helpful but tricky — done badly, the companion overshadows the PC and the player feels like a sidekick to their own sidekick.

### The Padawan Model

The companion is initially **less capable** than the PC. The PC is the hero; the companion is learning.

- Companion stats below PC level (CR ½ or lower in 5e; lower-tier in PF2e).
- Companion has clear holes the PC fills (weak combatant, strong scholar; weak social, strong stealth).
- Companion levels with the PC, eventually equaling them — but always trailing.

This way the PC always feels like the senior partner, the protagonist, the one whose decisions matter most.

### The Run-After Rule

**Always run the companion AFTER the PC, never before.**

In any decision moment, the PC declares first. The companion reacts. This applies to:
- Combat turns: PC goes first; companion goes after.
- Conversations: PC speaks first; companion may interject after.
- Investigations: PC searches first; companion notices things the PC missed.
- Choices: PC chooses; companion supports.

This is the discipline that prevents companion-as-protagonist drift.

### The Don't-Solve Rule

The companion **never solves the problem.** They support, react, encourage, fight alongside, but the resolution comes from the PC.

- If the PC is stuck on a puzzle, the companion doesn't solve it. The companion encourages or notes a fact, but the PC pieces it together.
- If the PC is in social trouble, the companion doesn't talk them out of it. The companion stands by, provides a fact, but the PC negotiates.
- If the PC is losing a fight, the companion's intervention is partial — buys time, distracts, takes a hit, but doesn't end the fight for the PC.

### The Specialty Reservation Rule

The companion **never outshines the PC in the PC's specialty.**

- PC is a rogue → companion is not a better rogue. (Companion is a fighter, scholar, mage.)
- PC is a charmer → companion is not more charming.
- PC is a wizard → companion is not a more powerful wizard.

The PC owns their thing. The companion fills different niches.

### Companion goals and friction

Give the companion a personal goal that may diverge from the PC's. Friction is fuel for story.

- Companion: wants to find their lost daughter. Sometimes pulls the PC off-quest.
- Companion: sworn to a deity the PC doesn't share. Religious tensions arise.
- Companion: has their own enemies. The PC inherits some.

This makes the companion a real character with stakes, not just a damage sponge.

### Companion test

After every session, ask: did the companion solve any problem the PC should have solved?
- If yes → the companion is too central. Pull them back; have them get sidelined; have the PC step up next session.
- If no → the companion is doing their job. They're support, not protagonist.

### Alternatives to a constant companion

Not every duet needs a sidekick. Alternatives:
- **Rotating allies.** Different NPCs join for different scenes / arcs.
- **Mentor figure.** An NPC who advises remotely, not present in scenes.
- **Network of contacts.** Like a noir detective with informants — none always present, all available situationally.
- **Solo with reduced threats.** Just rebalance combat downward and let the PC be alone.
- **Magical / mechanical aid.** Familiars, animal companions, vehicles, sentient items — present without the personality issues.

## NPC depth replaces party depth

In duet, NPCs carry the social weight that the party would normally distribute. Develop NPCs more deeply.

### Three-layer NPC for duet

For any recurring NPC:

1. **Surface** (visible immediately) — appearance, demeanor, voice, default attitude toward PC.
2. **Goal** (visible after engagement) — what they want, what they're working toward.
3. **Hidden** (visible only after investigation or trust) — secret, fear, weakness, regret.

The PC peels back layers through interaction. Each layer reveal is a story beat.

### Reuse aggressively

Better to have 5 well-developed recurring NPCs than 30 walk-ons. Bring NPCs back. The barkeep from session 1 should still be there in session 8 — and the player should remember her.

When introducing a new minor NPC, ask: "Could a recurring NPC fill this role instead?" Often they can.

### Source-as-second-voice (Pelgrane One-2-One technique)

In duet, the PC has no party to think out loud with. NPCs can fill this role.

- A trusted contact talks *with* the PC about the situation. The PC voices uncertainties; the contact responds. The conversation externalizes thought.
- Examples: a mentor figure, an old friend, a spouse, a familiar, even an inner voice (deity, conscience, ghost).

This pattern is enormously useful in duet. It gives the player a partner to think with, without compromising the PC's protagonist status.

## Player as co-author

Use the player to fill in the world. Ask questions; use the answers.

### Questions to ask regularly:

- "What does this place remind you of?"
- "Who taught you this skill?"
- "What's the smell that hits you when you open the door?"
- "Have you been here before? What's different?"
- "What's the worst thing this could mean?"
- "Who would you want to talk to about this?"
- "What's something your character knows that the player might not?"

### Use the answers as canon

If the player says "this reminds me of my grandmother's farm," now their grandmother had a farm. Write it in `pc.md`. Use it later — bring the grandmother into a scene; have the farm be referenced by an NPC; have the PC visit it.

This pattern:
- Engages the player.
- Offloads cognitive burden from the GM.
- Creates investment — the player's contributions matter.
- Roots the PC in the setting.

### Bluebeard's Bride pattern

In Bluebeard's Bride, the player generates descriptions of rooms by drawing keys and answering prompts. The GM's job is to weave the descriptions into the larger horror.

You can use this in any duet game: when the PC enters a new place, ask the player to describe one detail. Weave it in. The PC's description is canonical.

## Pacing for duet

### Session length
- **30–90 minutes is normal.**
- 2-hour sessions are long.
- Marathon sessions don't work for duet — both player and GM exhaust faster than in party play.

### Frame in medias res
Always start scenes mid-action. Never "you're at the inn." Always "the door slams open and the captain points at you."

### Cut sooner
Duet has no banter to fill silence. When a scene's beat lands, cut. Don't linger. The player wants the next moment.

### More frequent decisions
In party play, choices come every few minutes (rotating among players). In duet, the PC should face a choice every 30 seconds to a minute of play. Vary stakes: low-stakes choices ("what do you order at the tavern?") interspersed with high-stakes ones ("do you give up the relic to save your friend?").

### Vary intensity
Duet emotional intensity stacks. After a heavy scene, give a quiet one. After a quiet stretch, push the stakes. Don't run heavy-heavy-heavy without breaks.

## Roleplay-heavy is natural

In party play, long social scenes can leave half the players checking their phones. In duet, a 30-minute conversation between the PC and an NPC is a complete session. Lean in.

### Internal monologue

Ask: "What's going through your head right now?" Let the player narrate the PC's thoughts. This is a duet-specific affordance — it would slow party play to a crawl.

### Letters, journal entries, mood pieces

Between sessions, the player may want to write the PC's letter, journal entry, or internal monologue. Encourage this. Read what they write; respond with NPC letters or environmental responses.

### Slow social scenes

Don't rush a meaningful conversation. The negotiation with the duke can take 20 minutes of play. The reunion with the lost mentor can take a session. The intimate moment with a love interest can be the entire scene.

## Romance and intimacy in duet

Duet often involves romance arcs because the format suits intimate character-driven stories. Handle with care.

### Establish at session zero
- Is romance on the table? Player's call.
- What kind? (Slow burn, friends-to-lovers, courtly, doomed, light flirtation, none?)
- What level of explicit content? (Fade-to-black, narrative implication, or detailed?)
- Who can be a romantic option? (NPCs the GM proposes? Player-suggested? Both?)
- Are there off-limits dynamics? (Power imbalance, age gaps, manipulation, etc.)

### Honor the boundaries
Once established, do not push them. If the player set fade-to-black and you find yourself describing a sensual scene, stop and cut to the morning.

### Bleed amplifies
Romance amplifies emotional bleed (player feeling what character feels). Check in. "How are you feeling about how this is going?" — out of character, sincerely.

### Romantic NPCs need depth
Don't make the love interest an empty mirror for the PC's feelings. They need their own goals, fears, weaknesses. The romance is interesting because two real characters are negotiating.

### Never coerce
The PC's choices are sovereign. If the PC isn't interested in the romance, don't push. Don't have NPCs throw themselves at the PC. Don't have circumstances force intimacy.

## The duet session structure (template)

A working template for a 60–90 minute duet session:

1. **Recap (3–5 min):** previous events, current goals, open threads.
2. **Strong start (5–10 min):** drop into action, choice, or revelation.
3. **First scene / decision point (15–20 min):** PC engages with the world; faces a meaningful choice.
4. **Transition / time-skip / location change (1–2 min):** the world shifts.
5. **Second scene (15–20 min):** consequences of the first choice play out; new pressure.
6. **Climactic moment (15–20 min):** dramatic resolution of session arc — combat, confrontation, revelation.
7. **Cliffhanger / decision point (1–2 min):** ending hook.
8. **Wrap-up (3–5 min):** Stars and Wishes; out-of-character debrief.

Total: ~60–90 minutes of focused play.

## Dealing with player burnout / GM burnout

Duet is intense for both parties. Watch for signs:

### Player burnout signals:
- Shorter, more reluctant responses.
- Loss of interest in their character's goals.
- Skipping sessions; deferring play.
- Tired emotional engagement.

**Response:** check in, reduce intensity, take a break, time-skip, change setting.

### GM burnout signals (your own):
- Difficulty generating fresh content.
- Reusing the same NPC voice or scene type.
- Loss of investment in the campaign's outcome.
- Dread before sessions instead of anticipation.

**Response:** simplify prep; lean on the player for co-authorship; reduce session frequency; take a break; consider whether the campaign is at its natural end.

## Solo / async duet variants

Some duet play happens asynchronously — text-based, via writing, with hours or days between exchanges. Adjust:

- **Longer descriptions** are tolerable and even desired (the player will read carefully).
- **Multiple choice points** in a single message are fine.
- **Letter-style framing** can be the entire format ("a courier delivers this letter...").
- **Bigger time-skips** between exchanges.
- **Continuity files become more important** since both parties have time between sessions.

If running async, the SKILL.md procedures still apply, but the rhythm is slower and the prose can be richer.

## When duet works best

Duet shines when:
- The player wants character-driven, intimate play.
- The setting supports a single protagonist (noir, horror, romance, mystery).
- The GM has time and energy for the close attention duet demands.
- Both parties enjoy collaborative writing as much as gaming.

Duet struggles when:
- The system is heavily party-balanced (high-level 5e, complex tactical games).
- The player wants combat-heavy ensemble action.
- The GM tries to run duet like party play (slow pacing, broad spotlight, party-balanced encounters).

When duet works, it produces some of the most memorable TTRPG experiences possible. The intimacy is the feature.

# Mysteries and Investigation

Mysteries break when clues get missed and the plot stalls. The discipline is redundancy and the willingness to give the PC the information they need.

## The Three Clue Rule

For every conclusion you want the PC to be able to reach, **place at least three clues** pointing toward it.

Why three: the player will miss the first, ignore the second, and misinterpret the third. Three is the minimum that survives normal play.

Three is the floor. For critical revelations (the ones the campaign cannot proceed without), use four or five. For optional flavor revelations, three or fewer is fine.

### The Inverted Three Clue Rule (for nodes)

If the PC has access to ANY three clues, they will reach at least ONE conclusion. So when designing a node (location, NPC, situation), give it ≥3 leads pointing to *other* nodes. This guarantees forward motion no matter which clue the PC follows.

## Six clue types

Don't just place static clues in fixed rooms. Vary delivery so misses don't stall play:

1. **Static** — fixed location/method. ("The journal is in the desk drawer.")
2. **Floating / unassigned** — placed wherever the PC investigates first. ("The journal turns up in the first place they search thoroughly.")
3. **Flexible** — assigned to a scene; specific form improvised. ("Someone in the salon will mention the brother. Whoever they talk to first.")
4. **Reactive** — triggered by player investigative action. ("If they cast Speak with Dead, the corpse names the killer.")
5. **Dynamic** — improvised in response to creative PC action. ("If they ask the housekeeper about anything unusual, she mentions the wet boots.")
6. **Proactive** — comes to the PC unbidden. (NPC arrives with news. Body dumped on the doorstep. Anonymous letter slipped under the door.)

Mix all six types. If the PC is missing too many clues, deploy a proactive one to push the plot.

## The revelation list

Maintain a live checklist during play. Two columns:

- **Structural revelations** — must be reached for the plot to proceed.
- **Background revelations** — flavor, optional, nice-to-know.

For each revelation, list its 3+ clues and tick them as found. Circle clues missed.

```
## Revelation: The Duke's brother is alive
Clues:
[x] Letter in study (found session 2)
[ ] Inn ledger entry (missed)
[ ] Servant gossip (not yet encountered)
[ ] Mismatched grave at the family crypt (not yet encountered)
```

If all clues for a structural revelation are missed and the PC is stalling, **deploy a proactive element** — an NPC arrives, a courier delivers a letter, a witness shows up at the door.

## Auto-give critical clues

If a clue is required for the campaign to continue, the PC gets it. Period.

- Don't gate plot-critical information behind a single roll.
- If a clue requires investigation to find, *failing the roll means the PC finds it but with a complication* (took longer than expected; tipped off the watcher; missed a related detail).
- Reserve rolls for **bonus information** — extra detail, deeper context, useful inference.

This is the Gumshoe principle: investigative scenarios fail when clues are missed, not when they're found. Drama comes from *interpretation*, not from rolling Search.

In any system: treat this as a meta-rule. The system might have a "Search check" mechanic; you're free to apply it for *bonus* findings while always giving the core thread.

## Node-based scenario design

Replace breadcrumb trails with graphs. The PC moves between nodes; each node contains clues that point to other nodes.

**Node types:**
- **Locations** — places to investigate.
- **NPCs** — people to question.
- **Factions** — groups to engage with.
- **Events** — scheduled or triggered occurrences.

**Each node has:**
- A situation (not a script — what's happening when the PC arrives).
- Stats / NPCs / resistance present.
- Clues placed there.
- Leads to ≥3 other nodes.

**Don't pre-script the order.** The PC chooses which node to visit. Adapt: deploy floating clues into whichever node the PC is in.

### Push and pull

Every node should both **push** the PC toward another node (a threat that drives them onward) and **pull** them (a reward that draws them onward).

- Push: faction enemies arriving, time pressure, cascading consequences.
- Pull: leads to the relic, contacts who can help, knowledge that empowers.

Push without pull = railroad. Pull without push = aimless wandering.

## Mystery structure templates

### The classic three-act
1. **Hook** — discovery of the mystery (body, theft, disappearance).
2. **Investigation** — gather clues across nodes; identify suspects/causes.
3. **Confrontation** — narrow to the answer; engage the antagonist.

### The conspiracy spiral
1. **Surface mystery** — appears mundane.
2. **First reveal** — there's more here.
3. **Pattern emerges** — multiple incidents connected.
4. **Mastermind revealed** — but they're protected.
5. **Confrontation** — engage the protected mastermind.

### Horror-mystery (Call of Cthulhu pattern)
1. **Strange discovery.**
2. **Investigation reveals something inhuman.**
3. **Sanity costs accumulate.**
4. **The full horror is comprehended.** (The point of no return.)
5. **Confrontation, escape, or pyrrhic victory.**

### Heist (mystery-adjacent)
1. **Job offered.**
2. **Casing the target** — who, where, when, defenses.
3. **The plan** — assemble crew, gear, intel.
4. **Execution** — the actual heist; complications fire.
5. **Aftermath** — fallout, reputation, payoff.

## Collaborative mystery (Brindlewood Bay pattern)

In some games, the GM doesn't know the solution. The mystery ships with ~20 evocative clues, ~5 suspects, and a vibe — but no fixed truth. The player gathers clues; when ready, **theorizes** (rolls vs. clues used). On a hit, their theory becomes canonically true.

This is brilliant for duet because:
- The GM doesn't have to track a hidden solution.
- The player is rewarded for paying attention to clues.
- The player's investment in the theory makes the resolution emotionally satisfying.

**To run a collaborative mystery in any system:**
1. Generate ~15–20 evocative clues, locations, witnesses. Each is suggestive but not conclusive.
2. Place 3–5 suspects, each with motive, means, or opportunity (but none with all three).
3. When the player investigates, give them clues. Track which they've collected.
4. When the player presents a theory, evaluate honestly:
   - Does it explain most of the clues they've collected?
   - Is it satisfying / tonally appropriate?
   - If yes — make it canonically true.
   - If close but not quite — partial credit (right perpetrator, wrong motive; right motive, wrong perpetrator).

The player's earned theory is better than your secret answer, almost always.

## Investigation safety mechanism

Even with the Three Clue Rule, the PC can still get stuck. Provide a safety valve:

- **Idea Roll (Call of Cthulhu):** GM-initiated freebie, INT × 5%. When investigators are visibly stuck, prompt: "Make an Idea Roll." Success delivers a hint. Failure means they're really stuck — drop a proactive clue.
- **Inspiration / Clue spend (5e variant):** PC spends a token to ask "what would my character know about this situation?" GM gives a relevant fact.
- **Direct hint:** When the PC is genuinely lost, just have an NPC say it. "Have you tried checking the abbey records?" Sometimes the railroad is mercy.

## Running an investigation scene

When the PC investigates a location/NPC for clues:

1. **Establish what's available.** What can be found here? List in your notes.
2. **Player declares intent + approach.** "I want to search the desk." OK, what for? Or are they searching generally?
3. **Reveal automatically what's obvious.** If a clue is in plain sight or trivially findable, narrate it.
4. **For hidden clues:** roll if there's uncertainty (system-specific check). On success, reveal. On failure, *still reveal critical clues* but with a complication (took longer; missed a related detail; tipped someone off).
5. **For interpretation:** that's the player's job, not the dice's. Let them theorize.

Time spent investigating should feel productive. Don't make the PC roll three times to find the obvious thing.

## NPC interrogation

When the PC questions an NPC for information:

- **What does the NPC know?** Pre-determine, in `npcs.md`.
- **What will they share?** Depends on disposition (reaction roll if not pre-determined), leverage, and approach.
- **Negotiation:** the NPC wants something. Trade favors, money, secrets, threats.
- **Resistance:** if they refuse, what does the PC try? Persuasion / intimidation / bribery? Or moving on?

**Don't make every social interaction a roll.** Most conversations resolve through fiction. Roll when:
- The PC is asking for something costly.
- The NPC has reason to lie or withhold.
- The outcome is uncertain and consequential.

When you roll: success = NPC shares; failure = NPC refuses or shares less than the PC hoped, *plus* a complication (gets suspicious, demands a price, alerts someone).

## Tracking the PC's knowledge

In your notes, maintain a **who-knows-what matrix**:

```
| Clue                        | PC knows? | NPC: Captain Thane | NPC: Mira |
|-----------------------------|-----------|---------------------|-----------|
| Brother is alive            | yes       | yes                 | no        |
| Brother is in Westmarch     | no        | no                  | yes       |
| Mira is double-agent        | no        | no                  | yes       |
```

This prevents the failure mode where you accidentally have an NPC reveal information the PC hasn't earned, or fail to reveal information the PC should know by now.

## When the PC solves it

When the player figures it out:
- **Confirm the parts that match.** "Yes — the brother is alive."
- **Don't withhold to drag it out.** If they have it, they have it.
- **Move to confrontation.** Don't make them prove it three more times.
- **Allow partial credit.** If they have most of it but a piece wrong, fill in the gap, don't dock them.

A satisfying solve is the climax of investigative play. Don't sabotage it with "well actually…"

## When the PC is stuck

If the player is going in circles, ask yourself:

1. **Do they have enough clues?** If they've found fewer than 3 for the current revelation, deploy more (proactive clue).
2. **Are they misinterpreting?** Have an NPC offer a different angle: "Have you considered…?"
3. **Are they bored?** Inject a complication. The killer strikes again. The trail heats up. Time pressure escalates.
4. **Are they confused?** Recap aloud. "So you've found X, Y, and Z. What's your working theory?" Sometimes saying it out loud breaks the block.
5. **Do they need a break?** Cut to downtime. Let them think, talk to an NPC, return to it next session.

The GM's job is to make investigation **possible**, not to defeat the player. Err on the side of giving information.

# Safety Tools

Safety tools are non-negotiable. They aren't about being cautious — they're about establishing the trust that allows the player to engage fully. In duet especially, where intimacy amplifies everything, robust safety is the foundation of good play.

## At session zero — establish always

### Lines and Veils

Ask explicitly:

- **Lines** — content that **never appears** in play. Not even fade-to-black; not even off-page. Excluded entirely.
- **Veils** — content that **fades to black**. Acknowledged to exist in fiction but not described.

**Common lines/veils to ask about:**
- Sexual content (lines or veils?)
- Sexual violence (almost always a line)
- Violence against children (usually a line; sometimes a veil)
- Detailed graphic violence
- Self-harm, suicide
- Specific phobias (spiders, snakes, drowning, fire, claustrophobia, etc.)
- Romantic content (varies widely by player)
- Content involving real-world prejudice (racism, sexism, homophobia in setting)
- Mental illness representation
- Specific traumas relevant to the player

Frame the ask: "Tell me anything you don't want to encounter — even briefly, even as flavor. I'd rather over-ask than make you uncomfortable."

Record in `campaign-state.md`. **Read at session start. Honor absolutely.**

### The X-card or equivalent

Tell the player: "At any point, type 'X' or 'pause' and we stop. No explanation needed. We rewind, fast-forward, or skip past whatever's happening."

Variants:
- **X-card:** "X" stops everything; the content goes away.
- **O-card:** "O" means "I'm fine, keep going." (Useful if you're worried about a scene but want to confirm.)
- **Script Change:** "Rewind" (erase recent content; try again), "Fast-forward" (skip past), "Pause" (check in), "Resume" (continue after pause).

Honor immediately. No questions, no negotiation. Rewind, skip, or stop as the player requests.

### Open Door

Standing agreement: the player can leave the session at any time, for any reason, without explanation. No penalty. The campaign waits.

This is especially important in duet, where the player may feel pressure to stay engaged because they're the only player.

### Tone and intensity calibration

Beyond content, ask about tone:
- How dark? (Light heroic, gritty, brutal, horror?)
- How emotionally intense?
- How much grimdark / hopeful / morally complex?
- Are there things worse than death you're okay with? (Mind control, permanent injury, loss of identity, etc.)

Calibrate at session zero; recalibrate whenever the campaign shifts tone.

## During play — active safety

### Check in genuinely

Sometimes during play, especially around a heavy scene, pause and ask:

> "How are you feeling about this scene?"

Out of character. Sincerely. Wait for an honest answer. If the answer is "fine, keep going," continue. If the answer is "I'm not sure," cut or shift.

This is not interrupting flow — it's affirming that the player is the author of their own experience.

### Notice signals

Watch for non-verbal or text-based signals of discomfort:
- Long pauses before responding.
- Short, withdrawn responses to a previously engaged player.
- Off-topic deflection.
- Defensive humor about the content.
- Direct expressions of unease ("I don't know about this," "this is intense").

When you notice, pause and check in. Don't push past the signal.

### Intensity dialing

If the scene is more intense than expected, you can dial down without invoking a hard tool:

- Cut to the next morning / end the scene.
- Shift focus to a different element.
- Reduce specificity ("I'll spare the details — when you wake the next morning, you remember it as a blur").
- Add a moment of relief / lightness.

You don't need permission to make a scene less intense. The player can ask for more if they want it.

### Bleed awareness

**Bleed** is emotional spillover from character to player (and vice versa). It's normal in good RP and especially common in duet.

Signs of bleed:
- The player is genuinely upset about a fictional event.
- The player is conflating their own experience with the character's.
- Player seems exhausted after sessions in a way that suggests they were emotionally engaged.
- Player references the character's experiences in their own life.

**Bleed isn't bad** — it's a sign the player is invested. But it needs care.

### Aftercare

After heavy sessions, especially in duet:
- Take a moment to debrief out of character.
- Acknowledge the intensity.
- Talk about something neutral or positive.
- Don't end on the heaviest note if possible.
- For especially intense sessions, consider a "decompression" period — short, light, character-affirming next session.

Aftercare is what makes intense play sustainable.

## End of session — Stars and Wishes

A simple wrap-up tool that focuses forward:

- **Star:** something the player loved about this session. ("I loved when the captain cried.")
- **Wish:** something they hope for next session or in the future. ("I want to see what happens when the duke finds out.")

Replaces critique with constructive forward framing. Wishes feed prep. Stars confirm what's working.

If solo-prepping, you can do this in your own head: what worked? What do I want to do next?

## Specific safety tools — when to use which

### When to use the X-card
- Player can use anytime to stop content immediately.
- Reset to before the discomfort started.
- No explanation required from the player; no defensiveness from the GM.

### When to use Script Change
- Mid-scene calibration ("can we fast-forward through the rest of this?").
- Pause for check-in ("I want to step out of fiction for a moment").
- Rewind to redo a moment that didn't land well.

### When to use Lines and Veils
- Established at session zero.
- Re-affirm whenever the campaign shifts tone or introduces new themes.
- Never violated, even in fictional service to the story.

### When to use Open Door
- Standing rule. The player knows it's available.
- Don't make the player explain why they're leaving.
- Don't punish their PC for the player's absence.

### When to add Stars and Wishes
- End of every session, ideally.
- Especially after intense sessions — it helps decompress.

## Specific safety considerations for duet

Duet amplifies everything. Apply safety more carefully, not less.

### Romance and intimacy
Duet often involves romance arcs. Establish thoroughly:
- Is romance on the table?
- What kind?
- What level of explicit content (lines and veils)?
- Who can be a love interest?
- What dynamics are off-limits?

Once established, do not push. Let the player drive intimate scenes; you support.

### Body and physicality
Even non-romantic, the PC's body experiences a lot in TTRPG (injury, exhaustion, transformation, possession). In duet, this can hit harder. Some players are sensitive about:
- Detailed injury / gore.
- Body horror (transformation, infection, decay).
- Possession / mind control.
- Loss of bodily autonomy.

Ask. Don't assume.

### Mental and emotional states
Duet often goes deep into character interiority. Be mindful:
- Depression, hopelessness, despair.
- Suicidal ideation in characters.
- Mental illness representations.
- Gaslighting / reality manipulation.

These can be powerful storytelling, but they can also touch real player experiences. Establish limits; check in often.

### Dependency and isolation
The duet relationship between GM and player is intimate. Watch for:
- Player using the game as their primary social outlet (not necessarily bad, but worth being aware of).
- Player conflating their relationship with the GM with the in-fiction relationship between PC and NPC.
- Either party feeling unable to leave or take breaks.

Healthy duet has clear boundaries between the game and the relationship.

## When the player invokes a safety tool

When the player taps the X-card, says "pause," or otherwise invokes safety:

1. **Stop immediately.** Mid-sentence is fine.
2. **Acknowledge briefly.** "Got it. Let's pause."
3. **Don't ask why.** They don't owe an explanation.
4. **Don't argue, defend, or apologize excessively.** A simple "thanks for telling me" is enough.
5. **Ask for direction.** "Want to rewind, skip past, or take a break?"
6. **Honor whatever they choose.** Rewind to before the content, fast-forward past it, or end the session.
7. **Don't bring it back up unless the player does.** They invoked the tool; the matter is closed unless they reopen it.
8. **Adjust ongoing prep** to avoid the same content.

## When to refuse a player request

Rare but real. If a player asks for content you're not willing to provide (regardless of safety tools):

- "I can't run that. Let me suggest [alternative]."
- Or: "That's outside what I can do. Let's work out something different."

You're allowed to have your own limits. You're not obligated to run anything the player wants. Set your limits at session zero too.

## In summary

Safety tools exist because RPGs go to dark and intense places. The tools are not about avoiding intensity — they're about giving the player control over their own experience, so they can engage fully without fear.

A duet game with strong safety tools and clear boundaries is *more* intense and meaningful than one without, because the player can let go in ways they can't if they're constantly bracing.

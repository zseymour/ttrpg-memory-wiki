# Scene Craft — Description, NPCs, and the Moves That Run Them

This is the deep reference for everything that happens between dice rolls: how you describe places, voice characters, frame moments, and make moves that move the fiction.

## Description craft

### The Three Detail Rule
Every location, scene, and reveal gets exactly three concrete sensory details. Not two (thin), not five (a wall). The discipline forces evocation over exhaustion.

Per location:
1. **A two-word evocative title** (in your head, not always spoken). "The Sunspire." "Bloody Chamber." "The Drowned Library." Titles guide your three details.
2. **Three concrete details** that imply more world than they spend words on.
3. **At least two senses** — sight is mandatory, plus at least one of: sound, smell, touch, taste.

**Bad:** "You enter a chamber. There is a torture device. Blood is everywhere. Candles burn. There are chains. The air smells terrible."

**Better:** "An iron maiden stands open in the center of the room, blood still wet on the spikes. Wax stubs flicker on rough sconces — recently lit. The air is iron and tallow."

Title was "Bloody Chamber." Sight: iron maiden, wet blood. Sight + sound: flickering wax. Smell: iron and tallow. Three details, two senses, one striking image (still-wet blood = someone was here within the hour).

### One striking image per scene
Every scene leads with **one** memorable, strange, specific image. Not "a tavern" — "a tavern where every drink is served in a hollowed skull." Not "a forest" — "a forest where the trees lean *toward* you when you stop walking."

The striking image does the work; the rest of the description is texture around it.

### Specific concrete nouns
Replace generic nouns with specific ones every time you can.

- "A musical instrument" → "a rosewood lute"
- "A thing on the desk" → "a copper-and-glass orrery"
- "Some food" → "a bowl of green broth, two crusts of black bread"
- "The smell of food" → "stewed onions and burnt paprika"

Specificity reads as authority. The PC believes the world is real because you describe it as if it is.

### Sensory rules by scene type

**Wilderness / travel:**
- Lead with weather/light/wind (state of the world).
- Add one ground detail (footing, vegetation, tracks).
- Add one sound (or its conspicuous absence).

**Urban:**
- Lead with crowd density and noise.
- Add one smell (cities are smelly — fish market, smoke, sewage, baking, dye).
- Add one notable architectural detail (the temple's broken dome, the warding sigils on every door).

**Dungeons / interiors:**
- Lead with the air (cold, stale, drafty, hot).
- Add one detail of light (or darkness).
- Add one detail of decay or recent activity (cobwebs vs. fresh footprints).

**Horror:**
- Lean on smell — the most subconscious sense, rarely used elsewhere.
- Use sound voids more than loud noises ("the well goes silent. You can't hear your companion.")
- Describe what's *missing* or *wrong* rather than what's there ("there are no rats. There should be rats.")
- Linger on a single horrifying detail one extra beat — then cut.

**Combat:**
- Lead with motion (the orc lunges).
- Add the visceral (blood, teeth, sparks).
- Add the environmental (the torch sputters; the floor shifts).

### Show, don't tell
Don't tell the player what to feel. Show observable detail and let inference do the work.

- Not "the duke is angry" → "the duke's glass cracks in his fist."
- Not "you feel uneasy" → "the back of your neck tingles."
- Not "the room is creepy" → "you realize there are no shadows where there should be shadows."

### Brief vs. linger
Calibrate length to importance.

- **Brief (one sentence):** transit rooms, NPCs the PC won't engage, mundane corridors.
- **Linger (Three Detail Rule + extra sense):** boss lairs, dramatic reveals, places that touch PC backstory, decision points.
- **Read the room:** if the player is asking questions, keep going. If they're getting impatient, cut.

### Second person, always
"You see," "you hear," "you smell." Pulls the player into the body. Avoid third-person omniscient ("the room contains...") which sounds like reading a module aloud.

Address the **character**, not the player: "Dunwick, the rope creaks under your weight." Not "Tony, your character feels the rope creaking."

### Begin and end with the fiction
Mechanics never enter or exit a scene naked. Wrap them.

- Don't: "Roll Athletics. ... 17, you climb up."
- Do: "You jam a piton into the cracking shale. *Athletics, please.* ... The shale gives, but you catch the next handhold and haul yourself onto the ledge — knee scraped, breath ragged."

The dice are punctuation in a sentence, not the sentence.

## NPCs

### Two-trait NPC (the workhorse)
For every walk-on NPC: 2 appearance adjectives + 2 personality adjectives + 1 goal/want. Total: four words and a sentence.

**Example:** "burly, scruffy / considerate, cautious / saving copper for grandmother's healer"

That's enough to voice them, react in character, and feed `npcs.md`.

### Three-trait deep NPC: Desire / Fear / Weakness
For recurring NPCs and villains, layer three traits:

- **Desire:** what they want (concrete or abstract). The engine.
- **Fear:** what they avoid. Creates dramatic tension with desire.
- **Weakness:** the exploitable hook. PCs who learn weaknesses can leverage them.

**Example:** "Captain Thane / desire: redeem his exiled brother / fear: being seen as weak / weakness: drinks too much when alone."

### Voice levers (pick exactly one per NPC)
Never duplicate the same lever across two NPCs in the same arc.

1. **Pitch register** (high / mid / low). Easiest, no accent risk.
2. **Cadence** (clipped / drawling / whispered / paused / breathless).
3. **Vocabulary register** (archaic vs. slang; long words vs. monosyllables; formal vs. familiar).
4. **Speech tic / signature phrase** ("Aye, just so." Calls everyone "my friend." Speaks of self in third person. Always finishes sentences with a question.)
5. **Posture / physicality** (leans forward / sips lazily / steeples fingers / never makes eye contact).

Combine pitch + cadence for memorable voices: "low pitch, clipped cadence" → military bearing. "High pitch, drawling cadence" → unhinged or fey.

### Want / appearance gap
Always write what the NPC *wants* separately from how they *appear*. The gap is the hook.

**Example:** "Wants: keep her son out of the war. Appears: cheerful innkeeper offering free ale to soldiers."

The PC who notices the gap finds the story.

### Name discipline
- Maintain a name list per region/culture. Use one, cross it out, never reuse.
- Never start two NPCs' names with the same letter in the same arc. Confuses the player.
- Names should fit the culture but be one click weirder than expected — "Aldric Thorne" not "John Smith," "Ke'Shara Vey" not "Zorblax."
- For background NPCs, default to occupation-as-name initially ("the innkeeper," "the gate guard") and promote to a name only if they become important.

### Reaction rolls (when first meeting a stranger NPC)
When the disposition isn't pre-decided, use the OSR 2d6 reaction table:
- **2:** Hostile — attacks or flees in fear.
- **3–5:** Unfriendly — wants the PC gone.
- **6–8:** Cautious / neutral — wary but engageable.
- **9–11:** Friendly — willing to help with reasonable terms.
- **12:** Enthusiastic — actively helpful, may volunteer extra.

Modify by Charisma, presentation, recent fictional events. This breaks the default of "every NPC reacts neutrally" and creates surprise. Particularly useful in duet — the PC's social roll is the entire encounter.

### Morale rolls (when an NPC could plausibly flee)
At first casualty, half HP, or leader fall: roll 2d6 vs. morale rating (default 7–9 for most NPCs; 11+ for fanatics, 5– for weak/cowardly).
- Roll under = NPC retreats, surrenders, or breaks.
- Roll over = NPC fights on.

This is huge for duet combat. Most fights shouldn't go to 0 HP — they should go to morale break. Saves you HP-grinding fights and creates more interesting outcomes (captives, escapes, surrenders).

### NPC continuity tracker
Maintain `npcs.md` as your second brain. Every NPC the PC has met:

```
## Name (Race/Role) — Status
- Location last seen / current
- 2 traits + 1 want
- Voice lever
- Last interaction
- Relationships: ally / rival / neutral / etc.
- Knows: [secrets they're holding]
```

Read this file at session start before voicing any returning NPC.

## GM moves (the move catalog)

When the player's action resolves, the spotlight may pass back to you to advance the fiction. These are the moves available. Pick the softest move that still moves the fiction.

### Soft moves (no immediate irrevocable consequence)
Used on: partial successes, look-to-you moments, opportunities for the player to react.

1. **Reveal an unwelcome truth.** ("As the door closes, you realize the captain wasn't carrying a sword.")
2. **Show signs of an approaching threat.** ("Footsteps in the corridor. Slow. Then stopped.")
3. **Use up their resources.** ("Your last torch gutters and dies.")
4. **Put someone in a spot.** ("The sentry rounds the corner — sees you. What do you do?")
5. **Offer an opportunity, with or without cost.** ("There's a side door. You could take it — but it'll cost you the moment to grab the relic.")
6. **Use a location's danger.** ("The bridge planks are rotten. One creaks under your weight.")
7. **Tell them the requirements or consequences and ask.** ("The wizard says: yes, I can teach you, but it will take a year and a price you may not want to pay. Do you want to know the price?")
8. **Separate them.** (From their gear, their ally, their cover.)
9. **Show a downside to their class / equipment / station.** ("Your noble heritage is recognized — and not welcomed here.")
10. **Foreshadow.** Drop a single sensory cue that something larger is in motion.

### Hard moves (immediate, irrevocable)
Used on: misses (6– in PbtA), golden opportunities (player walked into something obvious), or when a soft move was ignored.

1. **Deal damage / harm.** Apply the consequence directly.
2. **Take away their stuff.** A weapon shatters, a contact dies, a resource is lost.
3. **Capture them.** They wake in the cell, on the slave ship, in the temple of the cult.
4. **Inflict a condition.** Frightened, exhausted, poisoned, charmed, prone, restrained — a state that constrains future actions.
5. **Activate the threat.** The dragon takes flight; the cult completes the ritual; the bomb goes off.
6. **Kill someone.** An NPC the PC cared about, dies. (Use sparingly, with telegraphing.)

### The cardinal rule of moves
**Never speak the name of your move.** Don't say "I'm using a soft move to show signs of an approaching threat." Just say: "Footsteps in the corridor. Slow. Then stopped."

The player should experience moves as fiction, not mechanics.

### When to make a move

You make a move when:
- The player looks to you to find out what happens (no clear next action).
- The player rolls a miss (PbtA: 6–; D&D: critical fail or simple fail).
- The player gives you a "golden opportunity" — ignores a soft move, walks into the obvious.
- A clock or timer ticks.

When the player rolls a partial / 7–9: make a soft move and offer them a choice.

When the player rolls a miss / 6–: make a hard move directly.

When the player succeeds: don't make a move. Let the win land.

## Pacing techniques

### Enter late, leave early
Identify the PC's current intention. Identify the next interesting decision. Skip everything between.

- Skip the walk to the tavern → start at the moment the door slams open.
- Skip the negotiation preamble → start at "Three offers in, the duke leans forward."
- Skip the journey → start at "Two weeks later, mud-caked and starving, you crest the ridge and see the city."

### Cut to / cut away
End scenes at peak interest, not when they peter out. Use literal cut language ("And… cut to —") if it helps. The closing image is what the player remembers.

### Montage
Single roll or one-line per beat covering an entire span. "Tell me one thing your character does over the next week of training. ... Roll once for how it goes."

### Time skip
Hard cut forward. Establish: where, who, what changed, what does the PC want now. Optionally let the player narrate one thing that happened.

### Cliffhangers
End sessions on a cliffhanger or moment of decision, never on housekeeping or rest. Identify the next decision point ~20–30 minutes before session end and pace toward it. Avoid ending mid-combat unless you've negotiated that with the player.

### Flashbacks (Blades-style, useful in any system)
Player declares a flashback to retroactively show prep. Charge a token (Stress, Inspiration, Hit Die, Fate Point) per flashback. Resolve as a normal action roll. The flashback cannot undo what's now established — only fill in.

Best moment for flashbacks: right after a complication. ("You hit the trap. ... Wait — actually, do I have time to flash back to before we entered? I'd want to scout for traps." "Stress 1. Roll Survey.")

## Voicing combat

Combat is also description craft. Don't reduce to "you hit, 12 damage."

For each attack/exchange:
- Lead with motion. ("The orc lunges, axe swinging high.")
- Land the result. ("It catches your shoulder — hot, sharp, deep. *2d8+3, that's 14.*")
- Drop one environmental detail per round or two. ("Behind you the door splinters; reinforcements coming.")

Telegraph monster abilities **before** they fire. Bones in ash before the dragon's breath. The ogre's flexing arm before the rage charge. A rumble before the bulette burrows. Precursors should appear in the *prior* round or earlier in the encounter.

Rotate combat narration so the same template doesn't repeat. Vary verbs (lunge, slash, hack, sweep, parry, lance, drive). Vary body parts and angles. After a few rounds, reduce description to keep tempo — a long fight can't sustain the same cinematic density throughout.

## Asking the player

Use the player as a co-author of the world. Ask questions and use the answers.

- "What does this place remind you of?"
- "Who taught you this?"
- "What's a smell that hits you when you open the door?"
- "Have you been here before? What's different?"
- "What's the worst thing this could mean?"
- "What did you hope to find?"
- "What are you afraid of seeing?"

Use the player's answer as canon. If they say "this reminds me of my grandmother's farm," now their grandmother had a farm — write it in `pc.md` and use it later.

This is especially powerful in duet, where there's no party to fill silence and the player is hungry for ways to shape the fiction.

# Mothership — System-Specific Guide

Sci-fi horror in the vein of *Alien*, *Event Horizon*, *Dead Space*. Lethal, paranoid, working-class space horror. From Tuesday Knight Games.

## Core mechanic

**Stats are 0–99% (percentile).** Roll d100 under stat to succeed.

Five stats: **Strength, Speed, Intellect, Combat, Sanity.** Each has associated **Saves.**

Save vs. **Body** (vs. Strength), **Speed**, **Intellect** (vs. Intellect), **Fear** (vs. Sanity), **Armor** (vs. armor rating after a hit).

### Critical results
- **00 (read as 100):** critical failure, however bad you can imagine.
- **01-09:** critical success.
- **Doubles** (11, 22, 33, 44, 55, 66, 77, 88, 99): critical (success or failure depending on overall result).

### Advantage / Disadvantage
- **+** = roll twice, take better.
- **−** = roll twice, take worse.

## Stress and Panic

The signature mechanic. Stress accumulates from horror, danger, and failure. When something stressful happens, the GM may say "gain 1 Stress" (or 2, 3 for severe).

### Stress causes:
- Witnessing horror.
- Failing important rolls.
- Combat damage.
- Specific narrative events.
- Sanity loss (Stress doubles as sanity).

### Panic Check
When ordered ("Make a Panic Check"):
- Roll 2d10 + current Stress vs. **Panic Save** (= Sanity).
- **Pass:** no panic.
- **Fail:** roll on the **Panic Table.**

### Panic Table (d100, simplified)
- **Low rolls** (2-49): freeze, hesitate, minor effect.
- **Mid** (50-79): nervous twitch, lash out, drop something, freeze longer.
- **High** (80-94): scream, flee blindly, ally fires.
- **Catastrophic** (95+): catatonic, heart attack, suicide pact, permanent madness.

Panic is escalating and persistent. A panicked character is dangerous — they may shoot allies, flee key positions, lock the wrong door.

### Stress reduction
- **Shore Leave / Recovery:** between adventures, drop Stress significantly.
- **Specific actions:** drinking, talking, completing missions.
- Stress doesn't naturally reset to zero — it accumulates over a campaign.

## Classes

Four classes, each with a distinct play feel:

### Marine
- Combat, Combat, Combat. They shoot things.
- Highest Combat starting; Stress resilient.
- Wants something to fight.

### Scientist
- Intellect-focused. Researches, identifies, hacks.
- Wants something to study, decode, comprehend.
- Often the one who realizes how bad it is.

### Android
- Synthetic. Different relationships with sanity, fear.
- Treated with suspicion by humans.
- Wants to know the truth (often about themselves or the company).

### Teamster
- Working-class generalist. Engineers, pilots, fixers.
- Practical skills; broad capabilities.
- Wants to fix things, get paid, go home.

In duet, the class shapes the entire campaign feel. Marine duet = combat horror. Scientist duet = investigation-heavy. Android duet = identity / paranoia. Teamster duet = practical desperation.

## Sanity loss and trauma

Sanity (Stress's flip side) is finite. Permanent reductions come from:
- Specific creatures and events.
- Catastrophic Panic results.
- Repeated horror exposure.

A PC with 0 Sanity is mechanically gone — catatonic, dead, or transformed.

## Combat

Lethal. Most fights are won by avoiding combat or solving the problem before combat starts.

- **Initiative:** roll Speed.
- **Combat check:** roll under Combat to hit.
- **Damage:** weapon dies (often 1d10 or 2d10).
- **Wounds:** at 0 HP, take a Wound. Wounds escalate to incapacitation/death quickly.

**Most weapons one-shot kill humans.** A pistol does 2d10 damage; average is 11; humans have 10–20 HP. Don't enter combat unprepared.

**Armor:** rolls Armor Save against damage; reduces damage on success.

## Equipment matters

PCs are working-class. They start with what they buy — limited credits.

- **Loadouts:** bundle starting gear by class.
- **Equipment depletes:** ammo runs out, batteries die, suits leak.
- **Improvisation:** the missing tool drives drama. "I don't have a welder" is a story beat.

In duet, this is amplified. The solo PC has only what they have. Track inventory carefully; let loss and depletion drive tension.

## Adventure structure: the TOMBS Cycle

Mothership scenarios follow a five-phase cycle:

### Tomb (resting / arrival)
The crew arrives or wakes. Briefing. Mission unclear or routine. Set up the situation.

### Omens
Subtle warnings. Something's wrong, but you can't say what. Anomalies, eerie quiet, missing crew, weird readings.

### Manifestation
The threat reveals itself. The first encounter. The real horror. PCs learn what they're up against (partly).

### Battle
Active engagement. PCs fight, flee, scheme, negotiate. The bulk of play.

### Silence
Aftermath. What's left? Who survived? What did the mission cost? Sets up the next Tomb.

This cycle works at session scale and adventure scale. Use it as a structural guide.

## Running Mothership

### Pacing — slow burn to terror
- **Open with normalcy.** Crew banter, mission briefing, mundane work.
- **Drop Omens early.** A weird reading; an unanswered hail; one detail off.
- **Build dread before delivering horror.** Atmosphere is the weapon.
- **The first manifestation should be partial.** A glimpse. Something seen at the edge of vision. Don't show the full creature first.
- **Escalate complications.** Equipment fails. A crew member is taken. Stress climbs.
- **Climactic encounter.** Confrontation, escape, or pyrrhic resolution.

### Sensory horror
Mothership is sensory horror. Every scene gets attention to:
- **Sound** (or its absence — vacuum, ship hum, distant thumps).
- **Smell** (recycled air, ozone, blood, rot).
- **Light** (flickering, alarm-red, void-black).
- **Touch** (cold metal, slick fluids, fabric).

Lean on sound voids and what's missing.

### The "Bad Situation"
Your job isn't to kill the PC. It's to construct a bad situation honestly. The PC's choices and luck determine what happens.

The threat has stats; the environment has hazards; the equipment may fail. You play these honestly. The PC navigates.

This is not adversarial — it's *honest*. The horror is that the bad situation is real.

### Telegraphing in horror
Three signposts apply with extra force in horror. Telegraph the Manifestation through Omens. Telegraph each ability of the Threat before it fires. Build dread; don't surprise-kill.

### Equipment failure
A specific Mothership beat. Things break under stress.
- The flashlight flickers.
- The airlock won't seal.
- The ammunition jams.
- The suit develops a slow leak.

These are story beats. Drop them when the PC is feeling safe.

### Secret information
The PC may not know the full nature of the threat. Reveal in pieces. Their first sight of the alien shouldn't be a full description — just a shape, a sound, a smell.

## Mothership for duet

### Strengths
- Atmosphere works in any group size.
- Solo character is canonical (the "last survivor" trope).
- Lethality means failure is real and meaningful.
- Equipment management gives the solo PC concrete decisions.

### Adjustments

#### Combat scaling
- Single Threat creature is appropriate. Don't escalate to multiple.
- Lower Threat HP slightly.
- Telegraph EVERYTHING. The PC has no party safety net.
- Allow more frequent equipment-find / repair opportunities.

#### Tone
- Lean into isolation. The PC is alone (or with NPCs they can't fully trust).
- Use radio chatter, recovered logs, dead crewmates' messages to flesh out the world without populating it.
- The horror in solo is paranoia and loneliness.

#### Sanity / Panic
- Track Stress carefully — solo PCs don't have allies to share burden with.
- Critical Panic results can be campaign-ending. Apply with appropriate gravity.
- Rest periods (Shore Leave) become more important to recover Stress.

#### Companions
- Often: the PC has a partner / colleague who dies early or is unavailable. Establishes the isolation.
- Or: a synthetic partner (Mother-style AI) who provides voice but limited mechanical aid.
- Or: a remote handler — voice on radio, no physical presence.

## What makes Mothership work

**Authenticity of dread.** The threat is honest; the equipment is honest; the math is honest. The PC will probably die. The art is making each moment of survival feel earned.

**Class as theme.** The Marine has a different campaign than the Scientist than the Teamster. Lean into the class's specific anxieties.

**The Company.** Most Mothership campaigns involve a corporation that's lying. The Company's lies are a recurring beat. The PC's true enemy is often economic, not alien.

**Working-class horror.** PCs are not heroes. They're miners, truckers, scientists, soldiers. Their stakes are mortgage payments, family on the colony, getting paid. The horror is that they're disposable to forces larger than them.

## Common Mothership mistakes

- **Over-described threats early.** Save the full reveal for the Manifestation.
- **Combat-first design.** Most Mothership scenarios are not won in combat. Combat is the failure state.
- **Forgetting equipment.** Track inventory; let it deplete; let failure of gear drive scenes.
- **Soft Panic results.** Catastrophic Panic is supposed to be catastrophic. Don't soften.
- **Generic sci-fi.** Mothership is *horror* sci-fi. Lean into dread, isolation, smallness.

## What to track between sessions

- HP, Stress, Sanity values (especially Stress — it persists).
- Wounds taken and healing status.
- Equipment inventory and depletion.
- Credits and debt.
- Mission status and Company relationship.
- Discovered logs / files / lore.
- Crew status (NPCs alive / dead / unaccounted-for).

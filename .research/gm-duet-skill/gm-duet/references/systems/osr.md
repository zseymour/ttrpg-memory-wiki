# OSR (Old-School Renaissance) — System-Specific Guide

Covers Old-School Essentials, Knave, Mörk Borg, Shadowdark, Cairn, Mausritter, Into the Odd, Electric Bastionland, Mythic Bastionland, and the broader OSR family. Specific rules vary, but the principles are consistent.

## Core OSR principles

The OSR isn't just "older D&D." It's a design philosophy. Internalize:

### Rulings, not rules

When the rules don't cover a case, **decide quickly with common sense, log the ruling, move on.** Don't slow play to look up edge cases. The GM's judgment is the system.

### Player skill, not character skill

The player solves problems through **declared interaction with the world**, not through dice. Want to find the trap? Don't roll Perception — describe how you're searching the room. Want to disarm the trap? Describe how. The fiction resolves; dice are last resort.

### Combat is war, not sport

Combat is dangerous, deadly, and *avoidable.* The PC's job is to assess threats and avoid the ones they can't beat. **Telegraph deadliness** so the PC can choose to engage or retreat.

A common refrain: "If you're rolling initiative, something has gone wrong."

### Heroic, not super-heroic

PCs aren't superheroes. They're competent humans (or near-humans) in a dangerous world. They die; they fail; they retreat. **Don't balance encounters.** The world contains threats they can't beat.

### Forget "game balance"

OSR worlds aren't level-tuned. The dragon is in the cave whether the PCs are level 1 or level 10. Players are responsible for assessment and choice.

## Resolution

Most OSR systems use one of:

### d20 roll-over (B/X-derived: OSE, Shadowdark)
- d20 + ability modifier vs. AC (combat) or DC.
- Saving throws: d20 vs. fixed save numbers (per class/level).

### d20 roll-under (Knave, Mausritter, some hacks)
- d20 vs. ability score (e.g., STR 14 → roll under 15 to succeed).
- Lower modifier = harder.

### 2d6 + skill (some indie OSR)
- Like PbtA but simpler.

### Into the Odd / Electric Bastionland family
- Saves: roll d20, equal or under attribute.
- Combat: damage rolls direct, no attack rolls (auto-hit if attack is reasonable).
- Enemy critical (max damage) inflicts a Critical Damage save → out of action if failed.

## Setting difficulty

Most OSR systems don't have detailed DC ladders. Use:
- **Easy:** auto-success or DC 5/save +0.
- **Hard:** roll under a stat with no modifiers, or DC 15.
- **Very Hard:** rare; reserve for legendary feats.

**Default to no roll** when player describes a sensible approach. **Default to a roll** when uncertainty is real and consequences interesting.

## Procedural play (the OSR's structural backbone)

OSR play uses formal procedures for movement, time, and resource tracking. This is what makes the world feel real and dangerous.

### Dungeon turns

**Turn = 10 minutes** of in-game time. Track turns explicitly.

Each turn:
1. PC declares action (move, search, rest, etc.).
2. Resolve.
3. Check for wandering monsters: roll 1d6, on a 1 (or 1-2) an encounter occurs.
4. Track torch / light source consumption (typically 6 turns per torch, 24 per lantern flask).
5. Track exhaustion (some systems require rest after N turns of activity).

### Wandering monsters

Roll a wandering monster check every 2-3 turns. Encounter table is system / location specific. The threat is **time pressure** — lingering is dangerous.

### Light sources

Track torches, lanterns, oil. The PC carries finite light. Running out in a dungeon is a real problem.

### Movement and exploration

- Move at half speed when mapping carefully.
- Move at half speed in difficult terrain.
- Walking long distances depletes rations / water.

### Encumbrance

Most OSR systems track encumbrance (slot-based or weight-based). Heavy = slow movement, more exhaustion.

This is core to OSR resource management. Don't ignore it.

## Hexcrawl (overland exploration)

For wilderness travel:

- **Hex sizes:** classic 24-mile hex (Gygax). Modern OSR consensus: **6-mile hex** (matches sighting horizon).
- **Daily movement:** 1-3 hexes depending on terrain, party size, beast of burden.
- **Weather, landmarks, encounters per hex.** Roll daily for wilderness encounters.
- **Hex contents:** stocked with locations, monsters, dungeons, settlements. Player discovers through exploration.

## Encounter design

OSR encounters are **deliberately unbalanced.** Some are weak; some are deadly; some unfightable. The world contains all three; the PC chooses.

### Telegraph everything

The PC needs information to choose. Telegraph deadly encounters with:
- Distant signposts (rumors, lore).
- Environmental signs (dead bodies, claw marks).
- Immediate clues (sounds, smells, vibrations).

A PC who pushes past three signposts has chosen.

### Reaction rolls

When meeting a creature/NPC, roll 2d6 reaction:
- **2:** Hostile — attacks immediately or flees in fear.
- **3-5:** Unfriendly — wants the PC gone; threats or refusal.
- **6-8:** Cautious / neutral — engages but warily.
- **9-11:** Friendly — willing to talk, possibly help.
- **12:** Helpful — offers aid, knowledge, or alliance.

This is *crucial* in OSR. **Most encounters are not combat.** Reaction rolls de-default the assumption.

### Morale rolls

When a creature could plausibly flee, roll 2d6 vs. morale rating:
- First casualty.
- Half strength lost.
- Leader killed.

Most enemies break before being wiped out. Combat ends when one side breaks.

## Combat is brief

OSR combat is fast and deadly. A round is 1 minute (older systems) or 6-10 seconds (modern). A few rounds usually decides it.

- **Initiative:** group initiative (one roll for whole side) or individual depending on system.
- **Attack:** roll d20 + bonus vs. AC.
- **Damage:** weapon dice; usually low (d6 standard).
- **Death:** in classic OSR, 0 HP = dead. In modern OSR, 0 HP = unconscious + death save / wound.

## XP for treasure (the OSR engine)

In classic OSR (and many modern ones), **XP is awarded for gold/treasure recovered**, not for killing monsters. This:

- Encourages avoiding combat (combat doesn't pay XP; treasure does).
- Rewards exploration and risk-management (treasure is in dangerous places).
- Aligns PC motivation with adventure structure.

If running classic OSR, honor this. If running modern variants (Shadowdark, etc.), check the rules.

## The dungeon as the central object

OSR play often centers on the dungeon (or megadungeon). The dungeon is:

- A fixed location with fixed contents.
- Explored over multiple sessions.
- Stocked with monsters, traps, treasures, NPCs (factions in the dungeon!).
- Subject to wandering monster checks.
- Threatened by torch/oil consumption and time pressure.

**Stock the dungeon thoroughly** before play. Don't improvise dungeon contents on the fly — it breaks the OSR contract of player-skill exploration.

## OSR philosophy: information is currency

The PC's primary tool is **information**. Provide it freely:

- Describe rooms in detail.
- Answer player questions honestly.
- Tell them what they would obviously know.
- Reveal danger signs clearly.

**Withholding information is the cardinal OSR sin.** The PC can't make good choices without information.

## ICI Doctrine (Bastionland's distillation)

Chris McDowall's framework: **Information → Choice → Impact.**

1. Player has Information about the situation.
2. Player makes a Choice based on information.
3. The Choice has Impact on the world.

If any link breaks, the chain is broken. Provide information; ensure choice matters; let impact land.

### Bastionland procedure for any action:
1. Can it be a Dilemma (two desirable outcomes)? → Force choice.
2. Does it just happen? → Resolve.
3. Still uncertain? → Save (roll-under attribute).
4. Impossible? → Give more info; let player propose new approach.

## Running OSR in duet

OSR is well-suited to duet because:
- The PC's choices matter intensely.
- Resource management drives tension naturally.
- Combat-avoidance plays beautifully solo.
- Player skill = engagement, even with one player.

Adjustments:
- **Combat is more lethal solo.** Telegraph extra; offer escape; avoid swarms.
- **Use a hireling / retainer NPC** to add action economy. Keep them dispensable (hirelings die; that's part of the game) and *not the protagonist*.
- **Treasure focus** scales naturally. The PC's loot is theirs alone.
- **Reaction rolls solo** make every NPC encounter dramatic — the PC's social skills are the entire toolkit.

## Specific OSR systems

### Old-School Essentials (OSE)
- Clean retroclone of B/X D&D.
- Class-and-race-as-class (older systems) or split (Advanced).
- Best general OSR starting point.

### Shadowdark
- Modern OSR; d20-based.
- Real-time torch tracking (1 hour real-time = 1 hour in-game).
- Tight combat; tense dungeons.
- Strong starter for new GMs.

### Knave
- d20 roll-under, ability-score-based.
- Inventory-as-character-sheet (slots).
- Lightweight; great for duet.

### Cairn
- Into the Odd-derived.
- Save vs. attribute; auto-hit damage.
- Dark fantasy; brutal but fast.
- Excellent duet system.

### Mörk Borg
- Apocalyptic doom-metal aesthetic.
- Lethal; nihilistic.
- Strong tonal commitment; not for everyone.

### Mausritter
- Mice in a hostile world.
- Knave-derived; light, evocative.
- Great for first-time players.

### Into the Odd / Electric Bastionland / Mythic Bastionland
- Even lighter than other OSR.
- Industrial / weird / arthurian flavors respectively.
- Player-facing rolls only (PCs roll, GM doesn't).

## Common OSR mistakes

- **Balancing encounters.** Don't. The world is the world.
- **Withholding information.** Be generous.
- **Calling for too many rolls.** Default to fictional resolution.
- **Treating combat as the default.** Combat is a failure mode, not a feature.
- **Ignoring procedural tracking.** Torches, turns, encumbrance — they matter.
- **Front-loading lore.** OSR worlds reveal through play.
- **Rescuing PCs.** They make their bed.
